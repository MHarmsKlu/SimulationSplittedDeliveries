# Inventory Reorder Point Models with Stochastic Lead Times and Split Shipments

## Probabilistic Lead Time and Demand Models  
Classic reorder point (ROP) calculations assume constant lead time and deterministic demand. Modern approaches relax these assumptions by treating lead time and demand as random variables. In such *probabilistic models, the reorder point is based on the *distribution of demand during lead time rather than a single average. For example, Eppen and Martin (1988) demonstrated that using a “standard” ROP procedure under stochastic lead times can lead to incorrect stock levels, and they proposed a *correct procedure* accounting for the full distribution of lead-time demand ([Determining Safety Stock in the Presence of Stochastic Lead Time and Demand | Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.34.11.1380#:~:text=We%20consider%20the%20problem%20of,A%20correct%20procedure%20is%20presented)) ([Determining Safety Stock in the Presence of Stochastic Lead Time and Demand | Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.34.11.1380#:~:text=When%20the%20parameters%20are%20unknown%2C,results%20in%20the%20Appendix%20indicate)). In practice, this means computing the probability distribution of demand over the lead time and choosing an ROP that covers a high percentile of that distribution (rather than just the mean). By modeling lead time as a random variable (often with a known probability distribution), one can calculate safety stock to buffer against variability. This safety stock is added to the mean lead-time demand, yielding an ROP designed to achieve a target service level (e.g. a 95% probability of no stockout during lead time). Such percentile-based ROP formulations are widely discussed in inventory literature ([Determining Safety Stock in the Presence of Stochastic Lead Time and Demand | Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.34.11.1380#:~:text=We%20consider%20the%20problem%20of,A%20correct%20procedure%20is%20presented)). In summary, probabilistic ROP models replace the naive formula (Average Demand × Lead Time) with a *service-level trigger*: reorder when inventory falls to a level that statistically covers demand over the (uncertain) lead time with the desired confidence. This approach is common in operations research for continuous-review $(Q,r)$ systems and forms the basis of many safety stock calculations in supply chain management.  

## Service Level and Percentile-Based ROP Methods  
An important theoretical improvement on the basic ROP is to explicitly incorporate a *service level constraint. Instead of minimizing cost alone, one sets the reorder point such that a minimum service level (fill rate or cycle service) is met. In academic models, this often translates to choosing $r$ (reorder point) as the $p$th percentile of the lead-time demand distribution, where $p$ corresponds to the desired service level. For instance, if demand during lead time is assumed normal, $r = \mu_{L} + z_p \sigma_{L}$ (where $\mu_{L}$ and $\sigma_{L}$ are mean and standard deviation of lead-time demand, and $z_p$ is the standard normal quantile for service level $p$). Chiang and Chiang (1996) adopt this kind of approach by imposing a **predetermined service level constraint* in their model ([Reducing Inventory Costs by Order Splitting in the Sole Sourcing Environment | Journal of the Operational Research Society
        ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jors.1996.43#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20examine,multiple%20deliveries%20can%20significantly%20reduce)). They formulate the inventory decision with a service level requirement and then minimize costs under that constraint ([Reducing Inventory Costs by Order Splitting in the Sole Sourcing Environment | Journal of the Operational Research Society
        ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jors.1996.43#:~:text=instead%2C%20considers%20the%20reduction%20of,between%20deliveries%20are%20determined%20optimally)). This percentile-based method ensures theoretical consistency with customer service goals and is frequently recommended in supply chain literature. Essentially, it formalizes the intuitive practice of adding “safety stock” such that the probability of stockout is kept below a threshold. By using statistical thresholds (e.g. 95th or 99th percentile of demand during lead time), the ROP becomes a function of both the demand variance and lead-time uncertainty, aligning inventory decisions with the chosen service level. Academic studies emphasize that this fractile method is more robust than the simple average-demand approach, especially when lead times fluctuate ([Determining Safety Stock in the Presence of Stochastic Lead Time and Demand | Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.34.11.1380#:~:text=We%20consider%20the%20problem%20of,A%20correct%20procedure%20is%20presented)).  

## Incorporating Split and Partial Shipments into ROP Calculations  
A growing body of research extends these models to situations where *replenishment shipments are split or delivered in parts*, rather than arriving all at once. Split shipments affect how we estimate effective lead time and safety stock, since a portion of the order may arrive sooner. Several theoretical frameworks have been proposed to integrate partial deliveries into ROP decisions:

•⁠  ⁠*Two-Stage (Two-Shipment) Models:* Moinzadeh and Lee (1989) considered an inventory system in which each order is potentially delivered in two installments. In their model, the supplier might ship part of the order immediately (a first shipment) and the remainder later (second shipment). The size of the first shipment is random, reflecting partial fulfillment due to stock limitations or replacements of defective items ([Approximate Order Quantities and Reorder Points for Inventory Systems Where Orders Arrive in Two Shipments | Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.37.2.277#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20consider,the%20approximate%20cost%20function%20are)). They derived operating characteristics and an approximate cost function for this system, and then optimized the order quantity and reorder point to minimize total costs ([Approximate Order Quantities and Reorder Points for Inventory Systems Where Orders Arrive in Two Shipments | Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.37.2.277#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20consider,the%20approximate%20cost%20function%20are)) ([Approximate Order Quantities and Reorder Points for Inventory Systems Where Orders Arrive in Two Shipments | Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.37.2.277#:~:text=exploited%20to%20bound%20the%20search,by%20means%20of%20numerical%20examples)). This work demonstrates how classic $(Q,r)$ policies can be adjusted when lead time is effectively split into two phases. The reorder point in such a case must ensure enough inventory to bridge the gap until the first partial delivery arrives (reducing the immediate risk of stockout), plus additional safety stock for the interval until the second shipment. Their results showed that accounting for split deliveries can significantly alter the optimal reorder point compared to the single-delivery assumption.

•⁠  ⁠*Multiple Partial Deliveries:* Extending the idea further, researchers have examined more than two shipments per order. *Chiang and Chiang (1996)* explored a continuous-review system with the possibility of multiple deliveries in each order cycle ([Reducing Inventory Costs by Order Splitting in the Sole Sourcing Environment | Journal of the Operational Research Society
        ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jors.1996.43#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20examine,multiple%20deliveries%20can%20significantly%20reduce)). Focusing on a sole-supplier scenario, they asked whether splitting every order into $n$ smaller shipments could reduce inventory costs. Using a service-level constraint (to ensure a given stockout risk is not exceeded), they found that optimally timing the inter-arrival of these partial shipments can *significantly reduce cycle stock and holding costs* ([Reducing Inventory Costs by Order Splitting in the Sole Sourcing Environment | Journal of the Operational Research Society
        ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jors.1996.43#:~:text=instead%2C%20considers%20the%20reduction%20of,between%20deliveries%20are%20determined%20optimally)). Essentially, by receiving smaller batches more frequently (within the same lead time window), the average on-hand inventory is lower while still protecting against stockouts. Their theoretical model determines the optimal number of shipments and timing such that total cost is minimized for a given service level. This framework shows that reorder policies can be refined to include an internal schedule of deliveries — an approach that generalizes the ROP concept when delivery is not a single event.

•⁠  ⁠*Stochastic Lead Time Distributions with Order Splitting:* Another line of research treats split shipments as a form of *risk pooling. By splitting an order into two or more parallel shipments (for example, ordering half the quantity from one supplier and half from another, or splitting an order into two batches sent sequentially), a company can reduce the variance of lead time. *Thomas and Tyworth (2006) provide a *critical review* of this “order splitting” strategy for lead-time risk pooling ([Pooling Lead-Time Risk by Order Splitting: A Critical Review - TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/784456#:~:text=After%20more%20than%2020%20years,promising%20research%20directions%20are%20suggested)). They summarize over two decades of literature showing that splitting orders among multiple suppliers can lower the probability of extreme lead time delays. In terms of ROP, this means the distribution of lead-time demand is tighter (less safety stock needed for the same service level). However, their review also points out practical limitations – for instance, the cost of managing multiple suppliers or shipments might outweigh the inventory savings in some cases ([Pooling Lead-Time Risk by Order Splitting: A Critical Review - TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/784456#:~:text=After%20more%20than%2020%20years,promising%20research%20directions%20are%20suggested)). Nonetheless, theoretical models (e.g. Lau and Zhao 1994, as cited in their review) formulate cost-optimal split proportions for dual sourcing, assuming arbitrary lead time distributions ([Supply Uncertainty and Diversification: A Review - SpringerLink](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-84628-607-0_15#:~:text=Supply%20Uncertainty%20and%20Diversification%3A%20A,split%20proportions.%20IIE%20Transactions%2026%3A66%E2%80%9375)). These models extend the basic ROP by determining what fraction of an order to route to each source (or each delivery batch) so as to minimize expected costs while maintaining service levels. The key insight is that *lead time is treated statistically and possibly as a mixture of distributions* – effectively a form of multi-modal lead time in the ROP calculation.

•⁠  ⁠*Service Levels for Partial Shipments:* Recent research has started to differentiate *safety stock allocations for each delivery in a split shipment scenario. For example, Sarkar and Giri (2022) consider a continuous-review system with stochastic lead-time demand where the replenishment is delivered in *multiple batches to the buyer ([Safety stock management in a two-stage supply chain with controllable lead time and batch shipments: a technical note | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377049983_Safety_stock_management_in_a_two-stage_supply_chain_with_controllable_lead_time_and_batch_shipments_a_technical_note#:~:text=buyer%20provides%20a%20certain%20range,rate%20depends%20on%20both%20price)). Uniquely, they introduce separate safety factors for the first batch versus later batches ([Safety stock management in a two-stage supply chain with controllable lead time and batch shipments: a technical note | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377049983_Safety_stock_management_in_a_two-stage_supply_chain_with_controllable_lead_time_and_batch_shipments_a_technical_note#:~:text=buyer%20provides%20a%20certain%20range,rate%20depends%20on%20both%20price)). The intuition is that the first partial delivery, which arrives sooner, should be calculated with one service target in mind (ensuring immediate needs are met), while subsequent deliveries (arriving later) cover the remaining demand. Their model optimizes the order quantity, the number of shipments, and both safety factors jointly to minimize total cost ([Safety stock management in a two-stage supply chain with controllable lead time and batch shipments: a technical note | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377049983_Safety_stock_management_in_a_two-stage_supply_chain_with_controllable_lead_time_and_batch_shipments_a_technical_note#:~:text=buyer%20provides%20a%20certain%20range,rate%20depends%20on%20both%20price)). This kind of framework generalizes the ROP formula by embedding two reorder thresholds: one that triggers the order (as in a standard $(r,Q)$ system), and implicit sub-thresholds that determine how much of the order must arrive in the first shipment to achieve a desired service level. It effectively integrates split delivery decisions into the reorder policy itself.

•⁠  ⁠*Hybrid Emergency Replenishment Models:* Another approach to partial shipments is found in hybrid inventory policies that combine regular orders with *expedited shipments. Roni *et al. (2016) propose a model with two demand streams – regular and surge demand – and correspondingly two supply options ([A hybrid inventory policy with split delivery under regular and surge demand | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/285044527_A_hybrid_inventory_policy_with_split_delivery_under_regular_and_surge_demand#:~:text=This%20paper%20proposes%20a%20hybrid,These%20equations%20are%20used%20to)). In surge situations, a portion of the reorder is satisfied via an emergency shipment (fast but costly), while the rest follows the normal lead time. This split-delivery policy is optimized using a Markovian analysis (level-crossing theory) and search heuristics ([A hybrid inventory policy with split delivery under regular and surge demand | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/285044527_A_hybrid_inventory_policy_with_split_delivery_under_regular_and_surge_demand#:~:text=lower%20than%20the%20volume%20of,shortage%20costs%20are%20relatively%20low)). The authors show that a policy which splits replenishment between an immediate (emergency) lot and a standard delivery can outperform a single-mode policy in terms of cost and service, especially when holding and stockout costs are low ([A hybrid inventory policy with split delivery under regular and surge demand | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/285044527_A_hybrid_inventory_policy_with_split_delivery_under_regular_and_surge_demand#:~:text=develop%20a%20search,shortage%20costs%20are%20relatively%20low)). The theoretical contribution here is an algorithm to decide *when and how to split an order* (triggering an emergency shipment for part of the order) based on the state of inventory and the demand surge. While not a simple closed-form formula, this approach provides a decision rule that effectively modifies the reorder point depending on real-time conditions (e.g. if a surge is detected, the “effective” lead time for a portion of the order is shortened via splitting). This demonstrates another way researchers incorporate partial deliveries: by layering an expedited supply channel onto the reorder policy. 

Each of these methods shows how *split shipments complicate but enrich the classic ROP model*. The common thread is that lead time is no longer a single number — it may be a distribution with multiple stages or sources. The reorder point must therefore account for the timing and quantity of partial arrivals. The literature provides various theoretical frameworks: from analytical cost-minimization formulas ([Approximate Order Quantities and Reorder Points for Inventory Systems Where Orders Arrive in Two Shipments | Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.37.2.277#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20consider,the%20approximate%20cost%20function%20are)) ([Approximate Order Quantities and Reorder Points for Inventory Systems Where Orders Arrive in Two Shipments | Operations Research](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.37.2.277#:~:text=exploited%20to%20bound%20the%20search,by%20means%20of%20numerical%20examples)), to percentile-based service calculations ([Reducing Inventory Costs by Order Splitting in the Sole Sourcing Environment | Journal of the Operational Research Society
        ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jors.1996.43#:~:text=instead%2C%20considers%20the%20reduction%20of,between%20deliveries%20are%20determined%20optimally)), to simulation and heuristic algorithms for complex cases ([A hybrid inventory policy with split delivery under regular and surge demand | Request PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/285044527_A_hybrid_inventory_policy_with_split_delivery_under_regular_and_surge_demand#:~:text=lower%20than%20the%20volume%20of,shortage%20costs%20are%20relatively%20low)). All of these move beyond the naive ROP = average demand × lead time, and instead use probability distributions and service level targets to ensure sufficient stock *despite uncertain and split lead times*. The consensus in academic supply chain literature is that by modeling lead time probabilistically and explicitly incorporating partial shipment dynamics, one can derive more reliable and cost-efficient reorder points that maintain desired service levels in practice.
